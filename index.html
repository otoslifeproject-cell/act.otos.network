Alcohol Care Team · S2 Liaison Psychiatry · Addenbrooke’s Hospital

You are reading this alongside CGL and CRS — each was sent their own version of it. Nobody has agreed to anything.

Helen — I told you that the next time we met I would not be your patient. I have been working towards that ever since, and this is what I built. You are seeing it before almost anyone else, because the bedside is where this started.

I now know that had my ADHD been treated earlier, I would have lost far less, suffered less harm, cost the system far less — and who knows, maybe even avoided the life-destroying results of addiction altogether. Now I have the personal proof and the evidence gathered, I want to intervene earlier — for the people caught in the same cycle, drowning in addiction while waiting for an ADHD diagnosis or medication.

This is not asking the bedside to become an ADHD service. The mission is simpler than that:

1 · The cohort you already see — adults repeatedly cycling through relapse, crisis, A&E and return, whose addiction may be ADHD-driven.

2 · A clinically governed priority-review route, by agreement — subject to all services’ agreed criteria, partner agreement and governance, rather than leaving people on the waiting list while addiction does its damage and crisis turns into costly consequence.

3 · Then hold them on the Continuity Layer — one tokenised Continuity Thread per person, and the thread follows the participant, not the service. It is consent-led throughout, and there is no write to anybody’s clinical record, no interface to the hospital system and no connection to the NHS Spine. It costs you one login — nothing to install, nothing else to learn.

None of that starts at number one. It starts with a question nobody has answered locally — and with one small piece of commissioned research, a report put to Cambridgeshire Public Health, that answers it. That report is stage one, and it is the only thing on the table today. What I need from you is your honest read on whether that question is worth answering — and, if you think it is, your say-so in your own words.

I have done a lot of work on this, and much of it I would rather say in the room than flatten onto a page. So I have written this to be read before we meet, not instead of meeting.

01

Thank you first.

You did the thing the system could not. You just had nowhere to send it.

You will remember some of this. It was the third time in three months, and the first time I was lucid enough to have a proper conversation with you. You saw my makeshift office set up on the bed — I was writing the plan while I was still on the ward. I came through your ward more than once, at the point where there was nowhere obvious left to send me. I needed one specific thing: the right wording in a discharge letter, so a psychiatrist could finally reassess and remedicate the ADHD underneath all of it.

You sent it to my GP electronically, promptly. You told me the email system your end is complicated — that it has to go out through your admin on a different system — and you sent it anyway, that day. You did everything right. And the thread still broke one door later: it landed at the surgery, nothing happened with it, and I collected it myself — and sent it on to the psychiatrist. Eight days later I was prescribed the medication. Your letter was one of the two conditions he had set. It did its job. It just did not travel on its own.

The other thing I remember is the conversation. Because I had already tried mental health, and already been through the drug and alcohol service, you agreed there was nowhere left to send me. You said it better than I ever have:

  • “You’re right. There’s nowhere for me to send you. I’d appreciate more places to signpost.”

Not because you did not care. Because the route did not exist. If “refer to Continuity” had been there the first time I walked in, it could have shortcut my entire journey.

As I left, I said something I have been working towards ever since. Next time we meet, I won’t be your patient. I’ll be your partner. This is me keeping that — and it is short, because I know what your shift looks like.

02

What you see first

Alcohol harm in front. ADHD self-medication underneath.

People like me rarely arrive through an “addiction door.” We arrive in crisis, desperate to get out, using A&E because it is the only door we can find. It is not the right route, and we know it — but panic does not queue for the right route. By the time I reached you the last time, I was there on purpose — but it took me seven detoxes to work out how.

When ADHD is left waiting and addiction services run on rigid criteria, people engineer worse symptoms to qualify for help. That is a system failure expressing itself through patient behaviour. It is not a moral failing. It is a design problem.

And by the time someone reaches you they have usually run the gauntlet: A&E first, then a ward, and detox somewhere beyond that. For a brain self-medicating untreated ADHD, the wait between those doors is exactly where people are lost.

That is not the group that matters most to you. Most of the people in front of you did not come about alcohol at all. They came in with a broken leg, pancreatitis, a seizure, an injury, a mental-health crisis. Then they cannot drink. Withdrawal appears. The alcohol history surfaces. The Alcohol Care Team gets called — and the real story comes out at the bedside, often more honestly than it ever reaches a referral form.

That is the true upstream opportunity, and almost nobody else in the system has it: dependence found through a real A&E attendance, in someone who was not looking for help with drinking, at the moment denial drops and the pattern is still hot — before they disappear back into the gap.

I think it is the single most significant opportunity your department has to make a significant impact upstream — and in the first phase it costs you one click.

In April 2026 an independent consultant psychiatrist wrote to the CPFT Adult ADHD Team about my care and named it in one sentence:

  • “Adult ADHD previously complicated with excess alcohol use to self-medicate inadequately managed ADHD symptoms.”

Read the order of it. Not ADHD plus alcohol, treated separately. ADHD — complicated by self-medication. That letter is about me, and it is medical correspondence about my own care — not proof that any of this works.

One man’s experience is not evidence about a population, and I am not going to pretend it is. That is why I think the question is worth asking. It is not why I think I already know the answer.

03

Why the good work does not hold

The order of operations, and it is not optional.

People are being asked to recover using the exact executive functions unmanaged ADHD has impaired — planning and sequencing, holding a strategy in mind, pausing before acting, tolerating distress that arrives fast, and repeating a dull routine until it sticks. Every one of those is the thing recovery runs on, and the thing ADHD takes away.

Read that as a model, not a verdict — people vary, and whether it holds in a real local caseload is exactly what a first piece of research would establish.

The medication does not do the recovery work. It creates the conditions where it can finally land. I know that from the inside: seven detoxes, repeated A&E presentations, a rehab that took my prescribed ADHD medication off me at the door because it is a stimulant. The recovery work was not wrong. My brain could not use it without the driver being treated.

And one study is worth your time. In an addiction service in Nantes, 61.3% of people already in treatment had improved six months after the ADHD underneath was diagnosed — and it was retrospective, single centre, with no control group — but in nearly a third of them the medication started late. So it was not only the drug. It was being found — and then somebody staying with it. The study shows the first half. The second half is what I want to test here.

And what it was like when it finally worked, in three words: stable, lifted, clear. That is the whole case, and it is one man’s.

START study · Nantes, France · n=93 · Addictive Behaviors 2026 · PMID 41218347 · retrospective, single centre, no control group.

04

Two trusts, one patient

The insight is sharpest at the bedside. It dies at discharge.

You know this one better than I do, because you stand in it every shift. You are CPFT staff, on the Addenbrooke’s site. Liaison psychiatry in an acute hospital is provided by the mental health trust — which is exactly why what you see at the bedside has nowhere obvious to go when the person walks out of an acute door.

Here is the same boundary from my side of it. My hospital’s patient portal tells me I am not on any waiting list. My ADHD medication review list is held by a different NHS trust, about a mile away. The portal is right about its own trust and blind to the other — and I am the only thing connecting them.

The system did not fail because nobody cared. It failed because nobody could see my whole journey, connect the pattern and intervene early enough around the real diagnosis. OTOS surfaces signals; the clinical teams make every decision.

Nothing is lying and nobody is negligent. The design simply has no field for the thing the patient is carrying. Nobody owns the risk in the gap — and every broken thread in this story is an upstream intervention waiting to happen.

—

The Continuity Infrastructure

A non-clinical layer that holds the thread between services.

Every service owns its own episode. Nobody owns the time between them. That is not a criticism of anyone — it is how the system is built. But it means the most dangerous period in this journey is the one period that belongs to nobody. It is not a gap in care. It is unowned risk.

OTOS asks no organisation to take on that risk, that liability or that caseload. It fills the space intelligently with continuous connection, pre-agreed service updates, and behavioural reinforcement with addiction education — so the gap stops belonging to nobody and becomes genuinely useful. The person is no longer stuck on their own. Not joining up the services — holding the person across them, and updating them with agreed, Continuity-only updates.

The clinical notes stay where they are. The continuity signal travels. Every drift signal requires a human review decision before any action. No autonomous clinical output.

It is consent-led: nobody is on it without agreeing to be. The person opts in, agrees the services can share access to their thread, and the thread only ever shows specific, pre-agreed information.

For the services, in practice: waiting-list silence becomes a signal instead of a blind spot, micro check-ins keep contact going between keyworker calls, and a person reviews every signal before anything is done.

05

What I built, and what it would cost you

Three steps. Each one earned by the one before it.

What OTOS Continuity is, and what it is not

✕ What OTOS is not

  • Not a clinical service. It does not diagnose, prescribe or treat.
  • Not an EPR or clinical record. No record access, no write-back.
  • Not a connection between services’ systems. No integration and no feeds.
  • Not a route for notes or case files to move between services.
  • Not a replacement for any existing service.
  • Not crisis care. It makes no safeguarding or clinical decisions.
  • Not surveillance. No device monitoring, no tracking.
  • Not a finished product. It is a bounded demonstrator, and any savings are modelled potential, not promised.

✓ What OTOS is

  • A non-clinical continuity layer around services that already exist.
  • Not joining up the services — holding the person across them.
  • Focused on one defined, costly cohort, carrying only signals agreed in advance.
  • The clinical notes stay where they are. The continuity signal travels.
  • Consent-led and tokenised. No NHS number required by default.
  • Handoff receipts — so you can see whether the next step actually happened.
  • Drift visibility turns silence into something a keyworker can review.
  • Authorised view. Separate record. Same thread. Partners see each other’s agreed status posts, never each other’s notes or systems.

OTOS surfaces the signals. The partner team makes every decision. Nothing changes in how you work. You just see more of what already happened.

Step one — and it is the only thing on the table today

Your honest read on whether the question is worth answering. I am proposing one small piece of commissioned research to Cambridgeshire Public Health: is ADHD materially under-recognised among people at the intersection of addiction and mental-health complexity in Cambridgeshire? If you think that question is real, saying so in your own words would carry more weight than anything I can write — and I would use it for that one purpose only, with your say-so. If you think I am wrong, I would far rather hear it from you now.

Step two — the pre-pilot, and your part is one button

  • If someone already on the pre-pilot turns up through A&E and reaches you, you log that they did. One tap. That is the whole thing.
  • You do not enrol anyone. The cohort is hand-selected and consented in advance by the services that already hold them.
  • You do not assess or diagnose anything, and there is no new paperwork and no new system to learn. Whether it carries any new risk for you is not mine to decide — that is your line manager’s call and your information governance team’s, and I would expect you to put it to them before you ever pressed it.
  • Minutes. Across twelve weeks. That is honestly the size of the ask, and I would rather understate it than sell it to you.

Think of it as neuroplasticity for the system. Just as a treated ADHD brain can finally form new patterns, a connected set of services learns too — every receipt and every reply teaches the route to hold the next person a little better than the last.

Step three — the pilot, and this is the part that is really for you

Only if the pre-pilot holds, and only on what it actually shows, the Alcohol Care Team gets the thing that did not exist for me: refer to Continuity. The button you could not press. It opens on evidence, not on a promise — which is exactly why step one matters.

Up to 50 adults · 12 weeks active, read at 3 and 6 months, where consent and governance allow. Decision-grade local evidence — not rollout, not proof.
